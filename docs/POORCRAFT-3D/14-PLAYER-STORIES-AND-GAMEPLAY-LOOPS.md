# Player Stories and Gameplay Loops

This document describes what POORCRAFT 3D must feel like in play. It is more
useful than a list of mechanics because it exposes whether systems actually
connect.

## The first session: “I can survive here”

The player arrives in a place with a readable silhouette: a valley, coast,
highland, forest edge, or abandoned road. They can immediately identify a few
nearby opportunities and threats. The opening teaches movement, gathering,
basic tools, shelter, food, and the first local landmark without a wall of UI.

Within the first session, the player should see at least one reason to move
beyond a starter hut: a cave mouth, river, ruin, distant light, trade path,
dangerous creature, or rumor. The world must invite a story before it asks for
dozens of recipes.

## The first ten hours: “I have a direction”

The player has a base and has sampled a meaningful path. They might be:

- following a river to establish fishing, a mill, a water transport route, or
  a workshop;
- exploring caves and ruins for rare materials, magical relics, or forgotten
  factions;
- helping or undermining a nearby settlement;
- learning the first ritual, dragon-related lead, or magical crafting chain;
- building a secure homestead with NPC help;
- trading goods between locations and learning which faction values them.

The player is not expected to have every system. They should understand that
several futures are open and that their chosen future has visible roots in the
world.

## The middle game: “People and places depend on me”

The player now has durable capability: a workshop, magical site, trade route,
settlement role, companion, or faction status. NPCs can ask for help because
their work, safety, resource needs, and conflicts exist in the simulation.

At this stage, a player decision should affect more than a reward screen. For
example: repairing a bridge changes trade access; backing a claimant changes
guards and laws; diverting a river makes a mill district viable; exposing a
crime changes relationships; teaching a companion a role changes exploration.

## The long game: “I am shaping a realm”

The player can create or capture meaningful political and physical power. They
may establish a capital, pledge to a faction, found a breakaway settlement,
build an industrial district, safeguard a magical region, organize defenses,
or negotiate a multi-settlement alliance.

Empire play must stay embodied. When the player taxes, recruits, trades,
fortifies, appoints, or declares policy, there must be roads, buildings,
people, resources, and consequences to visit in first person.

## Example player paths

### River engineer

Begins with fishing and a riverside shelter. Learns to shape banks and create
flumes. Builds mills, pumps, workshops, and valve-controlled systems. Becomes
valuable to a town, then controls a network of water-powered production and
trade. Can combine industry with magic rather than abandoning either.

### Dragon-bound mage

Finds ancient sites, studies relics, forms a relationship with magical
creatures, and learns rituals that change travel, defense, agriculture, or
territory. Their later realm may depend on sanctuaries, enchanted defenses,
dragon routes, and political fear or admiration.

### Border ruler

Starts as a fighter, builder, or local helper. Earns land through service,
trade, inheritance, diplomacy, rebellion, or conquest. Develops a settlement,
appoints residents, protects routes, manages faction pressure, and chooses how
strict, merciful, magical, or industrial the realm becomes.

### Explorer-merchant

Maps routes, discovers resources, learns what different factions need, creates
caravans or river routes, and profits through information and relationships.
Their empire can be commercial instead of militaristic.

## Session loop

```text
choose an intention -> travel through a readable world -> encounter a problem
or opportunity -> act with tools/people/place -> see a persistent consequence
-> return, plan, improve, or change direction
```

The loop must work at a small scale—food, shelter, a cave—and at a large
scale—trade route, castle policy, war, or expansion.

## Co-op story

Co-op should add shared specialization and shared memories, not repair a weak
solo game. Friends may explore together, run different paths, build a town,
join a faction conflict, or divide settlement roles. The authoritative host
ensures that a river, NPC decision, castle law, or machine outcome means the
same thing to every participant.
