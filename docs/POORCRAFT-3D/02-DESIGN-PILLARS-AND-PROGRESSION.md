# Design Pillars and Progression

## Pillars

### 1. Crafting is a beginning, not the whole game

Crafting opens capabilities. It should lead to exploration, production,
settlements, faction relationships, and political choices rather than an
endless list of recipes with no larger purpose.

### 2. The world explains the mechanics

Water moves because the land and flow model explain it. A machine works
because a visible resource network reaches it. A castle exists where terrain,
routes, defense, and resources make sense. NPC behavior should be explainable
by what a character knows, wants, saw, or was told.

### 3. Player paths are parallel and combinable

Magic, engineering, combat, exploration, commerce, and rulership are valid
primary paths. They may reinforce each other. A player can operate a valve-era
computer network while using magic, or build a dragon-focused realm with only
limited industry.

### 4. Castles are gameplay systems

A castle is not a decorative structure or a loot chest. It is a place with
gates, laws, residents, services, production, defenses, politics, and a
relationship to the surrounding territory.

### 5. Solo play is first-class

Solo players need meaningful long-term goals, useful companions, NPC
relationships, faction conflicts, exploration, and empire management. No
important layer should require a second player to become interesting.

### 6. Simulation is bounded and readable

The world can be deep without simulating every particle or every distant
citizen at full frequency. Active regions receive detail; distant regions use
deterministic summaries and catch-up. Budgets are visible and testable.

## Core loop

```text
observe -> explore -> gather -> build -> specialize -> organize people
-> establish production -> earn trust or create conflict -> claim influence
-> defend/trade/expand -> leave a visible history in the world
```

## Progression without a treadmill

Progress should unlock new decisions, not merely larger numbers:

- a tool unlocks a new resource or route;
- a workshop unlocks a production chain;
- a companion changes how exploration or combat works;
- a settlement unlocks labor, defense, and trade;
- a castle unlocks territory, diplomacy, and strategic pressure;
- an empire changes the map and creates new obligations.

There should be several satisfying stopping points. A player may complete a
personal story, build a prosperous city, master a technology or magic branch,
unify a faction, or pursue a multi-realm empire. The game should not require a
single arbitrary final recipe to feel complete.

## Beta journey

A new player should be able to:

1. Create a named world and understand the first survival tasks.
2. Build a shelter and a first useful production loop.
3. Discover a river and understand why it is valuable.
4. Harness that river with a machine without needing a debug explanation.
5. Travel far enough to find a clearly distinct castle or capital.
6. Meet residents whose jobs, laws, and reactions are visible.
7. Choose a technology, magic, exploration, or political direction.
8. Recruit a companion and see that companion help in the world.
9. Affect a faction through a witnessed action, service, trade, or conflict.
10. Continue the same world alone or with friends.

## System interlocks

Progression becomes coherent when each major system creates reasons to touch
other systems:

| System | Creates reasons to engage with |
|---|---|
| Terrain and rivers | building, fishing, transport, mills, settlement location, defense |
| Crafting | tools, construction, trade, magic, machines, quests |
| NPC work | settlement economy, services, faction trust, defense, companions |
| Castles | travel, laws, politics, markets, garrisons, diplomacy |
| Magic | exploration, combat, travel, agriculture, faction identity, construction |
| Industry | geography, logistics, jobs, trade, power, settlement growth |
| Empire | every prior system through territory, needs, policy, and consequence |

Whenever a new feature is proposed, it should add a real row or strengthen an
existing connection. If it only adds another item icon, it is content backlog,
not core progression.

## Pacing rule

Early game offers survival and curiosity. Mid game asks the player to choose a
relationship with a place or faction. Long game asks the player to accept the
responsibility of influence. The game must never force a player into empire
management merely because they built a furnace, but it must make empire play a
real aspiration for those who want it.

## History is player-authored

The seed has an old history: terrain, faction presence, ruins, resources, and
political tension. The player creates the new history. They can join, reject,
trade with, undermine, ally with, protect, or conquer factions—or stay
independent. There is no one mandatory plot that tells every player which
faction or path they must become.

NPC requests, faction opportunities, and city problems are therefore **reasons
to act**, not a replacement for the sandbox. They reveal what a place needs;
the player chooses whether and how to answer.
