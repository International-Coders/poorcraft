# Seeded History and City Growth

## The world has an old history; the player creates the new one

Every seed produces a different geography, distribution of factions, ruins,
routes, resources, sites, hazards, and political starting conditions. Existing
factions and lore provide a world that already has culture and tension, but
they do not dictate a single campaign. The player authors the important new
history through travel, alliances, trade, construction, magic, industry,
rebellion, conquest, or deliberate independence.

There is no requirement to join a faction. A player can become a merchant,
independent builder, explorer, mage, engineer, outsider, ally, or future ruler.
Faction affiliation changes opportunities; it is not the only valid game path.

## The opening fantasy: choose the best place to begin

The opening should feel like the player is reading a real landscape and making
a personal decision. They may settle near a river, on a defensible hill, by a
forest, near a cave, on a coast, beside a trade road, or far from political
pressure. The game provides information and dangers, but does not hand them
one universally correct starting plot.

Spawn safety still matters. The player should arrive safely, then have freedom
to relocate and choose their true beginning.

## Seeded geography rules

The seed determines macro geography, but generation uses coherent constraints:

- mountains vary by biome, climate, tectonic/height parameters, and seed;
- coasts naturally favor beaches, plains, marshes, forests, cliffs, or other
  seed/biome-appropriate land rather than a generic border;
- rivers create wetter corridors where river forests, temperate dense forests,
  jungle-like areas, wetlands, farms, and settlement opportunities can arise;
- caves, mines, ruins, magical sites, and resource deposits follow the same
  world logic instead of random decoration;
- capital locations are seed-determined but constrained by travel routes,
  terrain, resources, water, and faction identity.

The exact seed should always reproduce the same starting world. Player edits
and player-created history are then stored separately from the seed.

## One directly managed capital

The player chooses one main city/capital to manage in depth. This is the heart
of their realm and the place where detailed city decisions belong.

The capital has two inseparable representations:

1. **Physical city** — actual housing, roads, walls, workshops, farms, water,
   storage, citizens, guards, machines, magical sites, and construction.
2. **City oversight panel** — a readable visual summary of population, hunger,
   housing, health, work, supplies, safety, morale/loyalty, construction needs,
   production, and requests.

The panel is a window into the physical city, not a replacement for it. Clicking
“housing shortage” should show the district and people affected; approving a
project should create a visible construction plan, material need, worker task,
and result in the world.

The player may personally build a civic project piece by piece or commission
NPC builders after selecting a site/plan and supplying the necessary materials.
The same project must look, cost, and behave consistently in both cases.

## Bed, Work, and Idle boxes

For a city NPC to live naturally in a player-built or NPC-built place, the
player must designate three real, reachable anchors:

| Box | Player meaning | NPC use |
|---|---|---|
| Bed Box | A home/bed area with a resident capacity and quality. | Sleep, recovery, household assignment, and a safe return point. |
| Work Box | A workplace area with a job type, tools/stations, storage, and access. | Shift destination, task selection, production, repair, training, or guard duty. |
| Idle Box | A social/rest/free-time area such as a square, tavern, garden, wall walk, dock, or custom player space. | Eating, socializing, resting, waiting, recreation, and non-work schedules. |

The boxes are not invisible teleport points. They are player-set areas attached
to visible world spaces. Each must pass reachability, collision, ownership,
capacity, and safety checks. A player can build anything they want; these three
designations tell the simulation how people can actually use it.

## NPC requests are the city’s voice

NPCs and groups raise needs through believable requests:

- more stone, wood, tools, food, beds, or workers;
- a bridge, well, road, wall repair, workshop, school, temple, laboratory, or
  market;
- protection from monsters, raids, hazards, radiation, flooding, or faction
  conflict;
- trade supplies, diplomatic support, or specialized magical/industrial help.

The player may solve a need personally, assign workers, trade for it, use
magic, build machines, change policy, postpone it, or refuse it. Consequences
must be understandable. A city grows stronger because needs were met through
real world action, not because a number was clicked.

## Satellite cities and political relationships

The player does not micromanage every city in the world. Other cities are
political entities with their own faction identity, leaders, needs, and degree
of autonomy.

| Relationship | What the player can do | What remains autonomous |
|---|---|---|
| Main capital | Directly plan, build, appoint, set policies, and respond to detailed requests | Citizens still have needs, opinions, and credible reactions |
| Allied/friendly city | Trade, aid, negotiate, protect, influence, and receive limited opportunities | Faction identity, local rule, and most internal decisions |
| Puppet/protectorate | Support, request cooperation, influence policy, provide defense/resources, and help it grow | Local culture/faction identity and day-to-day management; it remains a puppet unless released |
| Conquered city | Rebuild, set its puppet relationship, assign strategic role, and help it grow | It develops as its own city under delegated rule unless the player later releases it |
| Rival/hostile city | Trade covertly, negotiate, raid, defend, compete, or wage war | Its people and faction pursue their own goals |

This structure gives empire growth meaning without forcing the player into a
spreadsheet for twenty identical cities. It also preserves the identity of
friendly or puppet factions instead of repainting every place as the player’s
color.

## Ideology and new factions

The player may eventually found a new faction, but it begins by choosing an
ideological foundation from the values already alive in the world. That choice
creates a legible social contract: what the realm prizes, how it treats work,
magic, technology, war, outsiders, and local autonomy. The faction can develop
its own history and mixed identity through player choices; it is not a blank
repaint of a pre-existing city.

See `21-FIRST-PERSON-WAR-KARMA-AND-SERVER-SCALE.md` for the first-person war,
construction, and karma contracts.

## City strength

City strength is not one score. It emerges from population, food, housing,
health, morale/legitimacy, skilled workers, infrastructure, defenses, routes,
resources, faction relationships, magical/industrial capacity, and local
safety. Stronger soldiers, better services, and expansion are outcomes of
these systems working together.

Named NPC death is permanent. Losing a resident removes their skills,
relationships, work output, and possibly a military role. The city becomes
weaker until it trains, recruits, or reorganizes; losing guards directly reduces
available garrison strength. The oversight panel must show these losses and the
physical city must make them visible through missing workers, closed services,
or thinner patrols.

## Sandbox engagement rule

The game remains engaging because there are always several meaningful angles:
improve the capital, investigate underground, expand magical knowledge, build
industry, explore a seed-specific frontier, trade, respond to city requests,
work with or against factions, improve satellites, or create a new ambition.
The game must never require all players to pursue the same angle.
